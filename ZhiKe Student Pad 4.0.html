ZhiKe Student Pad 4.0

Getting students to study on their own, on a tablet their teachers didn't trust.

Over 7 years, ZhiKe's student tablet had split into 9 versions, all built for a teacher-led classroom. With my mentor, I was one of two UX designers who spent 6 months redesigning it around self-study, without making it feel like a game to the teachers and parents who decide whether students get to use it.

Role UX Designer (1 of 2)
Duration 6 months
Team Developers, Product Managers, UI Designers

TL;DR

Schools were moving toward self-directed learning, but the pad still assumed the teacher drove every task. Our research showed the bigger obstacle wasn't the students. About 80% of teachers were skeptical of the pads, and some locked them in cabinets. About 60% of parents worried that screen time was replacing study.

So we built motivation that adults could trust: the home screen is a to-do list instead of app icons, points come from finishing tasks rather than time on the device, and rewards are cosmetic only. Underneath, one flexible grid replaced the 9 separate homepage versions.

The Solution

Motivation that a teacher or parent can look at and trust.

A to-do list instead of app icons.

The home screen opens on today's tasks, both teacher assignments and goals students set themselves, so the first thing a student sees is work to do.

Points for finishing tasks, not for screen time.

Badges reward class participation and finished work, and leaderboards track study habits rather than grades.

Rewards that change how it looks, not how it works.

Avatars and themes are cosmetic only, and they expire, so they motivate without becoming something to grind for.

Progress that students, teachers and parents can all see.

Each gets a view of study habits and goals, while student privacy is kept.

The same pad for every student.

One interface across devices and subscription tiers, so no student gets a lesser version.

Under the Hood

One flexible grid instead of nine homepages

We replaced 9 separate homepage versions with one grid framework, working closely with developers to fit it onto legacy code.

Before: nine versions

  • Nine homepages to maintain
  • Students saw different apps depending on their version
  • Every update slowed down by legacy code

After: one framework

  • 60% less maintenance time
  • One consistent experience across versions
  • A base that new features can build on

What I Learned

Gamification for kids needs a brake as well as an engine.

Every reward we designed came with a limit: points for finished tasks rather than time, cosmetic rewards that expire, and natural places to stop.

Old software comes with habits.

Students and teachers knew the old layouts, so modernising 7-year-old code meant changing the structure without breaking what people already relied on.

In education, the user isn't the only one deciding.

Parents and teachers decide whether a student gets to use the pad at all, so their concerns shaped the interface as much as the students' needs did.
